Can Hearing Aids Help with Tinnitus? How They Work and Who They Suit

2 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: May 21, 2025 Read the original (한국어) ↗

Today's topic is a question many people ask: "Do hearing aids help with tinnitus?" Let me explain simply.

What is tinnitus?

Tinnitus is hearing a sound — a "beep," a "hum" or a "whoosh" — inside the ear when there is no actual sound outside.

It tends to stand out in quiet places, and it can cause poor sleep, difficulty concentrating and anxiety, which affects quality of life considerably.

How can hearing aids help with tinnitus?

Hearing aids don't just make things easier to hear. Hearing aids may help some people with tinnitus and hearing loss by improving access to surrounding sounds and communication. Reduced listening effort or tinnitus awareness may also ease distress. These possible benefits do not mean that hearing aids reliably reverse the brain changes associated with tinnitus or treat anxiety and insomnia on their own.

1. Improved access to surrounding sounds

You may have heard that tinnitus is more noticeable the quieter it is. Hearing aids amplify surrounding sounds so that the tinnitus is relatively less audible. This is called sound enrichment, and it naturally draws attention away from the tinnitus.

2. Possible changes in tinnitus perception

Reduced auditory input is one proposed contributor to tinnitus-related changes in auditory processing. Hearing aids improve access to sound, and some people find their tinnitus less intrusive. The mechanisms and individual benefit vary; this does not show that hearing aids reliably reverse the brain changes associated with tinnitus.

3. Optional built-in sound features

Many modern hearing aids have a tinnitus masking feature. They play soft sounds such as white noise or ocean waves that cover the tinnitus. Some manufacturers also offer smartphone sound-therapy apps that work with the hearing aids.

4. Potential support for communication and coping

Better hearing may make communication easier and may reduce tinnitus-related distress for some people. This is not guaranteed, and anxiety, insomnia or persistent distress may need additional assessment and treatment.

What does the research say?

The 2024 VA/DoD tinnitus guideline suggests hearing aids for tinnitus management in adults with hearing loss. This is a conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence: hearing aids may reduce the functional impact of tinnitus, but benefit varies between people. A summary of the guideline was published in JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery in 2025. The research suggests hearing aids can help reduce the day-to-day difficulties caused by tinnitus.

Who should consider hearing aids?

If a hearing assessment confirms hearing loss, hearing aids may be worth discussing for tinnitus management. Tinnitus that is worse in quiet settings or accompanied by anxiety or poor sleep should also prompt a broader management discussion; these symptoms alone do not establish a need for hearing aids.

A hearing test first shows whether you have hearing loss and how much. For the evidence on hearing aids in general, see hearing aids for mild to moderate hearing loss; for psychological approaches, see tinnitus CBT and self-help.

In closing

Hearing aids are not just a tool for hearing better. They can also help with easing tinnitus, emotional stability and quality of life.

Tinnitus is not something you simply have to put up with — active management can help. How much it helps differs from person to person.
