Mild or Moderate Hearing Loss: Do You Need Hearing Aids? What the 2026 Cochrane Review Says

8 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: September 2, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

When we discuss hearing aids in clinic, patients often say:

"I can still hear most conversations. Do I really need hearing aids already?"

"I'm fine somewhere quiet, but in restaurants or at gatherings I can't follow what people say."

"Isn't it better to wait until my hearing gets worse?"

Because people with mild or moderate hearing loss can still manage everyday conversation, many put off hearing aids. On August 26, 2026, Cochrane — one of the organizations that evaluates medical evidence most rigorously — published an updated review on exactly this question. Here is a plain-language summary, and what it might mean for you.

The short answer Whether to get hearing aids should be decided not only by the numbers on your hearing test, but by how much your hearing is affecting your conversations and daily life right now.
Find the cause first if you have
  • Sudden hearing loss in one or both ears
  • A large difference in hearing between the two ears
  • Persistent tinnitus in one ear, or pulsatile (heartbeat-like) tinnitus
  • Recurrent or persistent dizziness
  • Ear discharge or ongoing ear pain
  • Hearing loss together with changes in facial movement
In particular, if your ear suddenly feels blocked and the hearing in one ear drops, this is not the time to shop for hearing aids for age-related hearing loss. If hearing drops suddenly over 3 days or less, seek urgent ENT or emergency assessment today; recent sudden hearing loss should be assessed within 24 hours. New facial weakness or other possible stroke symptoms require emergency care. The UK NICE guideline on adult hearing loss (NG98) also recommends urgent specialist assessment for recent sudden hearing loss, and further investigation for one-sided or asymmetric hearing loss.

What did the 2026 Cochrane review look at?

This review updates a 2017 Cochrane review. It analyzed 16 randomized controlled trials with a total of 2,261 adults with mild to moderate hearing loss — 11 more studies than in 2017. Average participant age ranged from 58 to 83 years across studies, and study length from 4 weeks to 3 years. The studies were done in the US, Europe, Brazil, Hong Kong and Australia.

The researchers compared people using hearing aids with people who had no hearing aids, placebo devices, education programs or auditory training.

Everyday listening difficulty decreased

People using hearing aids had a significant improvement in their ability to hear and follow conversation in daily life. Statistically this was a "large" effect, with moderate-certainty evidence.

One point to note: this "listening ability" is based on questionnaires answered by the participants themselves. It does not mean that speech discrimination scores measured in a test booth rose by the same amount. The accurate reading is: the listening difficulties people experience in real life are substantially reduced.

"Moderate certainty" means the true effect is likely to be close to the estimate, but the result isn't completely definitive because of design limitations (for example, participants inevitably know whether they are wearing a hearing aid). In medical research, this is a reasonably reliable level.

Conversation and social participation improved markedly

Hearing loss is about more than audiogram numbers. Talking less with family, avoiding group conversations, missing the moment to laugh because you missed the joke — this sense of gradually dropping out of life is often the biggest burden of hearing loss.

In this review, difficulty taking part in conversations and social activities improved substantially. The authors concluded that the evidence is consistent with the current practice of widely offering hearing aids as a first-line option for adults with mild to moderate hearing loss who seek help for hearing difficulties.

The effect on overall health-related quality of life (questionnaires covering physical and mental health broadly) was smaller, and the evidence weaker. Hearing aids can change the hearing-related parts of life a great deal, but they are not a cure-all for life as a whole.

Will hearing aids prevent dementia or improve memory?

Honestly, this needs to be read carefully. As research on the link between hearing loss and cognitive decline has grown, many people have heard that "hearing aids prevent dementia." But hearing loss being associated with cognitive decline and dementia risk is a different question from whether wearing hearing aids prevents dementia.

In this Cochrane review, the effect of hearing aids on working memory was very uncertain, and no clear effect on loneliness was found (again, with low-certainty evidence, so "no effect" can't be concluded either). The current evidence does not support saying that hearing aids prevent dementia or improve memory, and the authors call for longer studies on cognition.

So the reason to recommend hearing aids is not "dementia prevention," but to hear better and take part more fully now — and that alone is well supported.

Are there side effects?

Six of the 16 studies (1,186 people) recorded adverse effects. One study reported two participants stopping hearing aids: one because of pain while wearing them and one because of ear dryness requiring treatment. No noise-induced hearing loss was reported, but adverse-event evidence was limited. But because few studies recorded side effects systematically, we can't say there are none. In practice, ear soreness, ear canal eczema, earwax build-up and feedback whistling are not unusual. Most can be fixed with adjustments, which is one reason regular check-ups after fitting are needed.

The decision isn't based on test numbers alone

We look at your hearing test results together with your real communication difficulties, your environment and the situations you want to improve. Someone who talks easily in a quiet consulting room may still be significantly affected in daily life if these happen often:

  • Hard to make out speech in restaurants or cafés
  • Family say the TV volume keeps going up
  • You often have to ask "Sorry, what?"
  • Hard to follow when several people talk at once
  • People seem to mumble

Guidelines likewise recommend offering hearing aids not at a fixed dB threshold, but to adults whose hearing loss affects their ability to communicate and hear other sounds (alarms, environmental sounds, music). The criterion is whether communication is affected, not a number.

Ear and hearing assessment comes first

If you've noticed hearing loss for the first time, the ears and hearing should be assessed before choosing a hearing aid. We examine the ear canal and eardrum (otoscopy) and assess the degree and pattern of hearing loss with pure-tone audiometry and, where needed, speech audiometry and tympanometry. This helps us find out:

  • whether the loss is in one ear or both
  • whether it is a sound-conduction problem (conductive) or an inner ear / hearing nerve problem (sensorineural)
  • which frequencies are affected
  • how well you distinguish speech, even when sound is audible (speech discrimination)

A treatable cause — impacted earwax, middle ear infection or Eustachian tube dysfunction — may be hiding, and expectations for hearing aids depend on speech discrimination ability. This assessment is what lets us decide properly whether hearing aids are right, and which kind. If both ears could benefit, we also decide together whether to use one or two, based on hearing and lifestyle. Two hearing aids are generally recommended for hearing loss in both ears, as they help with understanding speech in noise and telling where sounds come from.

With hearing aids, fitting matters more than buying

Two people with the same audiogram can need different amplification, tolerate loud sounds differently and struggle in different settings. A hearing aid isn't finished once you choose a product — it needs to be adjusted to your hearing and fine-tuned repeatedly based on real-life use.

A face-to-face follow-up 6–12 weeks after fitting is generally recommended to check comfort, sound quality, volume settings and handling, and to confirm the hearing aids are meeting your needs. The first few weeks are also when the brain adapts to new sounds, so adjustments during this period strongly influence long-term satisfaction.

When should you consider hearing aids?

You don't need to wait until your hearing is very poor. If testing confirms mild or moderate hearing loss and it is causing real difficulty in conversation or social life, it's reasonable to discuss hearing aids. On the other hand, a slight drop on a hearing test doesn't mean everyone needs hearing aids immediately. What matters is the combination of hearing test results + speech understanding + difficulties in daily life + your own needs.

Summary

The message of the August 2026 Cochrane review is clear: even in mild to moderate hearing loss, hearing aids are likely to meaningfully improve everyday listening difficulty and participation in conversation and social life. But the evidence is not yet enough to expect dementia prevention or memory improvement. So rather than asking "should I wait until my hearing gets really bad?", ask "how much is my hearing getting in the way of my conversations and daily life right now?" If the answer is "quite a lot," a hearing test is the first step.
