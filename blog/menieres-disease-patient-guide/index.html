Dizziness & Balance

Ménière's Disease: A Patient Guide to Symptoms, Causes, Treatment and Diet

4 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: December 29, 2023 Read the original (한국어) ↗

Ménière's disease is an inner-ear condition in which several symptoms appear together. It was first described in 1861 by the French physician Prosper Menière. This guide explains the typical symptoms, what is thought to cause it, how it is treated and what you can do day to day.

For a shorter overview focused on the four core symptoms, see Ménière's disease: the four symptoms that come together.

Symptoms

Hearing changes

The typical first symptom is difficulty hearing low-pitched sounds, such as a bass line or a deep voice. Over time, higher-pitched sounds such as birdsong or a phone ringing can also become harder to hear. Occasionally the hearing problem starts in the high pitches first. Hearing change is one of the most common symptoms of Ménière's disease. It usually begins in one ear, but as the disease progresses both ears are often affected.

Spinning vertigo

Another symptom is an attack of intense, spinning dizziness (vertigo). It comes on suddenly and usually lasts from 20 minutes to 12 hours, sometimes with nausea or vomiting. Dizziness lasting only seconds to a few minutes points first to other causes such as BPPV.

Diagnosis is based on the pattern of repeated attacks, a hearing test and exclusion of other causes. Definite Ménière’s disease typically requires at least two spontaneous vertigo episodes lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours, documented low- to mid-frequency sensorineural hearing loss in the affected ear and fluctuating ear symptoms. Probable Ménière’s disease can include episodes lasting up to 24 hours. Symptoms alone do not confirm the diagnosis.

Tinnitus

Ringing or other noises in the ear (tinnitus) are common. Their loudness and pitch can change with the state of your hearing.

Ear fullness

A blocked, full feeling in the ear (aural fullness) is also common and often comes as a warning sign before an attack.

Urgent and emergency signsSudden hearing loss needs urgent assessment, ideally the same day. If dizziness comes with sudden slurred speech, weakness, double vision, inability to walk or a sudden severe headache, call the local emergency number (119 in Korea) immediately. Do not assume this is a Ménière’s attack or wait for a clinic appointment.

Causes

The exact cause is not fully known, but several possibilities have been studied.

  • Inner-ear fluid (endolymph). Endolymphatic hydrops is an increase in inner-ear fluid volume and is associated with Ménière’s disease. It is thought to play a role, but the underlying cause of the disease is not fully understood. It tends to become more common and to progress with age, and can affect both ears.
  • Autoimmunity. When the immune system attacks the body's own tissue, this may also be linked to Ménière's disease. Studies suggest that certain immune-related substances, or reactions to particular inner-ear proteins, may be involved.
  • Stress and overwork can trigger or worsen symptoms. In women, symptoms may relate to the menstrual cycle. How stress hormones affect the disease is not yet clear, but they are thought to play a role.
  • Other health problems — such as too much sodium (salt) in the body, systemic metabolic disorders or an underactive thyroid — may also be linked.

Treatment

A few facts are worth knowing before treatment starts. In Ménière's disease, vertigo attacks often become less frequent over time, but hearing loss can progress, so regular hearing tests are needed.

The pattern also differs from person to person. Some people have frequent attacks, others only occasional ones. Treatment is planned around how often and how severe the attacks are, how much hearing has been lost, and whether one or both ears are affected. The approach also differs between the acute phase (when attacks are severe) and the chronic phase (when the disease has continued for a long time).

The role of medicines is still debated. They can help a great deal in relieving vertigo during an acute attack, but how well they protect hearing or slow the disease is not yet clear. Diuretics or betahistine can be used as maintenance treatment, but in a large study betahistine did not reduce attacks more than a placebo. Medicines are therefore used selectively, according to each person's response.

Daily habits that help

Reducing very salty foods and discussing possible triggers such as caffeine or alcohol are commonly recommended. Some studies suggest benefit, but the evidence is limited and responses vary. Agree any sodium target or major change in fluid intake with your clinician, particularly if you take diuretics or have another medical condition. Food choices and cooking methods matter:

  • Choose fresh food. Processed foods and fast food usually contain a lot of salt. Choose fresh fruit and vegetables, whole grains, and lean meat or fish instead.
  • Cook at home. Cooking yourself lets you control the amount of salt. Use herbs and other seasonings instead of salt for flavour.
  • Check food labels. When shopping, read the label and choose products lower in sodium (salt).
  • Flavour without salt. Use herbs, spices or other suitable flavourings instead of adding salt. Do not assume that low-sodium salt or a salt substitute is suitable for you: some contain potassium. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before using them, especially if you have kidney disease or take medicines that can raise potassium.

Small changes like these can help with managing symptoms — and a healthy diet is good for your overall health, not only for Ménière's disease.

Stopping smoking is also important for general health. Stress can affect symptoms too, so try to reduce it where you can. Getting enough sleep matters as well: good sleep habits improve overall health and can help in managing symptoms.
