Sleep & Snoring

CPAP and a Blocked Nose or Dry Mouth: What to Check First

12 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: October 24, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

These are typical things people on CPAP say: “My nose is completely blocked every morning.” “I wake up with my mouth so dry that I reach for water first.” “My nose feels stuffy, so I take the mask off before dawn.”

A blocked nose and dry mouth are common with CPAP. In our earlier article on CPAP types and National Health Insurance rental in Korea we said common side effects and how to manage them would come next. This is that article.

In short, a stuffy nose and dry mouth during CPAP use can be thought of as having three main sources:

  • Air leaking: the mask does not fit, or the mouth falls open during sleep so air escapes through the mouth.
  • Dry air: humidification is insufficient or the room air is dry.
  • The nose itself: rhinitis, a deviated septum (the wall in the middle of the nose is bent) or enlarged inferior turbinates can make the nose narrow.

These three are often tangled together. Rather than changing one thing and giving up on the grounds that “CPAP doesn’t suit me”, it is important to check them one by one. Dry mouth can also be caused by medicines you take or by other conditions, so it cannot be assumed to be only a CPAP problem.

Check first: when not to adjust things yourself and see a doctor
  • Repeated nosebleeds
  • Blockage on one side only, bloody nasal discharge or foul-smelling discharge
  • A blocked nose that continues for more than a few weeks despite adjusting the mask and humidification
  • Your nose is so uncomfortable that you use CPAP for fewer and fewer hours, or have stopped altogether
  • Snoring, pauses in breathing or daytime sleepiness have come back even though you use CPAP
  • Mask marks on the bridge of the nose or face that look like sores
CPAP only works if it is used regularly. If discomfort is reducing your use, that is itself a sign that you need a medical review.
If a nosebleed will not stopIf bleeding is heavy, you feel faint or have difficulty breathing, call 119 immediately; do not wait for a clinic appointment or drive yourself. Otherwise, sit leaning forward and pinch the soft part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes. If bleeding continues, seek emergency assessment.

1. Why does the nose block and the mouth dry out?

CPAP delivers air at a steady pressure through a mask to keep the airway open, as it tends to narrow during sleep. Because a large volume of air passes through the nose all night, the lining of the nose can dry and become irritated. This can cause nasal congestion, dryness of the nose and throat, and a sore throat.

Air escaping through the mouth deserves particular attention. In an experiment in which 6 healthy adults wore a nasal mask, opening the mouth to create a leak greatly increased the airflow resistance of the nose (how blocked it is). Using warm, humidified air greatly reduced this response.

A possible cycle is that mouth leak produces high one-way airflow through the nasal passages, which may dry the lining and increase nasal resistance, encouraging further mouth breathing (Richards 1996). This mechanism was observed in a short experiment in healthy adults and does not explain every case of nasal congestion.

Dry mouth is usually part of the same loop. If the mouth falls open during sleep and air escapes through it, the mouth and throat are very dry in the morning.

2. A checking order at home: from the mask to the nose

The order below is an example arranged to make checking easy. It does not mean you must go through every step before seeing a doctor.

Do not change the treatment pressure or the auto-pressure range yourself; the prescribing clinician decides this after reviewing your usage data. Adjust humidification and tube temperature according to the device manual and training, and if discomfort continues, contact the prescribing or rental provider.

① Is air leaking from the mask? Is the size right?

  • Air coming out of the normal vent holes of the mask or connector is not a fault. Do not block the vents with tape or anything else.
  • If air blows toward the eyes or around the edge of the mask, or you hear a hissing sound in your sleep, check for an unintentional leak.
  • Overtightening can make the cushion fold and leak. The principle is to tighten the straps only as much as needed to stop the leak.
  • A worn or hardened cushion seals less well. Ask your rental provider when to replace it.
  • Many devices and apps record leak data. Looking at it together at your appointment is helpful.

② Is the mask type right for your situation?

  • There are nasal masks, nasal pillows that fit into the nostrils, and masks that cover the nose and mouth together (oronasal masks).
  • In an analysis pooling 13 randomised and non-randomised studies (4,563 people), oronasal mask users needed a higher pressure than nasal mask users and used CPAP on average 48 minutes less per night (Andrade 2018). This is an association and does not mean any individual will differ by 48 minutes.
  • This does not mean oronasal masks are bad. For someone whose nose is so blocked that they can only breathe through the mouth, an oronasal mask can be a realistic choice.
  • It is best to choose with your prescribing clinic according to your nose and how much your mouth opens. After changing masks, check leak, residual apnoea events and comfort together.

③ Are humidification and tube temperature appropriate?

  • Heated humidification can help reduce dryness of the nose and mouth. In a short crossover study of 38 people, dryness of the mouth, throat and nose was less than without humidification, and use was about 35 minutes longer per night (Massie 1999).
  • However, feeling more comfortable and using the device for longer are separate things. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline found that humidification reduces side effects such as dryness, but could not confirm that it meaningfully increases hours of use (Patil 2019).
  • If humidification is too weak the nose dries out; if it is too strong or the room is cold, water droplets form in the tube, which can gurgle or splash onto the face.
  • The right setting differs with the season and room temperature. Adjust according to the manual and training, and contact the prescribing or rental provider if discomfort continues.

④ Is the room air too dry?

  • Winter heating and air-conditioner draughts blowing directly on you make nasal dryness worse.
  • Place the device where air does not blow directly on it, and check the bedroom humidity once with a hygrometer.
  • Conversely, air that is too humid increases mould and house dust mites, which can trouble people with rhinitis, so avoid excessive humidification too. See also rhinitis and humidity.

⑤ Does your mouth fall open during sleep?

  • If dry mouth is severe in the morning and the device’s leak record is high, suspect mouth opening.
  • Approaches include changing the mask type and using a chin strap. However, one analysis found the evidence that these approaches increase hours of use is still insufficient (Mansell 2023, systematic review).
  • So rather than insisting on one method, check whether discomfort and hours of use actually improve after a change.

⑥ What is your nose like to begin with?

  • If your nose was often blocked before you started CPAP, there may be a problem with the nose itself, such as rhinitis, a deviated septum or enlarged inferior turbinates.
  • In that case, changing the mask and humidification can only go so far, and the next step is to look at the nose directly.

3. Can I use a nasal spray?

Different kinds of nasal spray are completely different in nature.

Nasal steroid spray

  • It is a maintenance treatment that settles inflammation of the nasal lining caused by conditions such as allergic rhinitis. It does not open the nose immediately; it works when used regularly.
  • It is not habit-forming, but it is not free of side effects. Local effects such as nasal dryness or nosebleeds can occur.
  • In an analysis pooling 3 randomised studies (224 people) in CPAP users over 4 weeks, differences in hours of use and overall nasal symptoms were not clearly significant (Segsarnviriya 2025, an update of a 2017 analysis).
  • This does not mean treating rhinitis is useless; using it is reasonable for people in whom rhinitis has been confirmed.

Decongestant nasal sprays (vasoconstrictors)

  • They open the nose quickly, but long daily use can cause rhinitis medicamentosa, in which the nose becomes more blocked (Zucker 2019 systematic review).
  • If you depend on this kind of spray every night because of CPAP, have your nose checked rather than continuing on your own.

Saline irrigation is a supportive method to remove secretions and crusts. Use sterile saline made for nasal rinsing, or make the solution with distilled water, sterile water, or water that has been boiled and cooled, following the product instructions. Do not use untreated tap water, and wash and dry the rinsing device after use.

This does not mean no nasal spray should ever be used daily. The problem is long-term continuous use of decongestant (vasoconstrictor) sprays. See also choosing a nasal spray for a blocked nose.

4. What does an ENT doctor check?

The role of an ENT consultation for CPAP-related nasal congestion is to find out what is actually inside the nose.

  • Nasal endoscopy: a thin endoscope is used to look directly inside the nose for a deviated septum, enlarged inferior turbinates, nasal polyps, dry or bleeding areas of the lining, and sinusitis discharge.
  • If needed, allergy testing or a sinus CT may be used to look further for causes.
  • If nosebleeds recur, the bleeding site is found and treated.

Once the cause is confirmed, the direction of treatment is decided:

  • If rhinitis is the cause: medication and day-to-day management.
  • If the nose is structurally narrow: surgery can be discussed when symptoms match the examination findings and appropriate medical treatment has not helped enough.

There is an analysis pooling studies of nasal surgery performed on its own in people with sleep apnoea who have nasal obstruction (Camacho 2015, 18 studies, 279 people). After surgery the CPAP pressure needed was lower (7 studies with pressure data, 82 people), and device-measured hours of use also increased (33 people, followed for less than 6 months). However, the studies were mainly non-randomised, so the size and durability of the effect are uncertain. These are results from some patients with clear nasal obstruction; they do not mean everyone needs surgery.

The 2023 guideline developed through the Korean Society of Otorhinolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery, with participation from the Korean Society of Sleep and Breathing, takes a similar approach. When people cannot use CPAP well because of nasal obstruction and symptoms continue despite appropriate medical treatment, referral for nasal surgery can be considered to increase CPAP use. The recommendation grade is “Option” (Park 2023). Nasal surgery does not replace treatment of sleep apnoea itself; think of it as playing a supporting role that helps people use CPAP better.

A retrospective study reported that people who stopped CPAP early after starting had higher nasal resistance, and suggested that nasal disease be appropriately treated before and after starting CPAP (Inoue 2019). However, nasal status in that study did not predict whether people were still using CPAP a year later, so it cannot be said that treating the nose guarantees long-term use.

5. Look at it together with your hours of use

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) clinical practice guideline strongly recommends providing education on how to use CPAP when starting. It conditionally recommends behavioural support and troubleshooting to resolve problems early in treatment, and interventions in which usage data are reviewed remotely and help is given when needed. As good practice, it also states that follow-up after starting should review objective data, not only hours of use but also treatment effect, leak and residual apnoea events (Patil 2019).

Rather than simply putting up with discomfort, looking at usage data together with symptoms and reducing causes one by one helps people keep using CPAP for the long term.

To be honest, there is not yet sufficient evidence to guarantee that side-effect management measures such as humidification, chin straps, nasal sprays or changing masks increase hours of use for everyone. Another analysis noted that studies differ in their participants and measurements, making conclusions hard to draw (Mansell 2023). These findings leave uncertainty about improvements in CPAP use; they do not prove that every approach is ineffective. They also do not establish which patients will benefit. Symptom relief and hours of use should therefore be reviewed separately when choosing an approach with the prescribing clinician.

Summary: checking a blocked nose and dry mouth on CPAP
  • A blocked nose and dry mouth on CPAP are common; air leak, dryness and the nose’s own condition are often tangled together.
  • Example order: mask leak and size, mask type, humidification and tube temperature, room humidity, mouth opening, then the nose itself.
  • Do not block the normal vent holes.
  • Do not change treatment pressure yourself; the prescribing clinician decides. Adjust humidification and tube temperature according to the manual, and contact the prescribing or rental provider if you are uncomfortable.
  • Avoid long-term continuous use of decongestant (vasoconstrictor) sprays; use nasal steroid sprays when rhinitis is present.
  • Repeated nosebleeds, one-sided blockage, blockage lasting more than a few weeks, and falling hours of use are signs you need a medical review.
  • A nosebleed that does not stop after 10–15 minutes of pressure needs an emergency department; if bleeding is heavy, or you feel faint or have trouble breathing, call 119.
  • A nasal endoscopy to look at the structure inside the nose helps decide the direction of treatment.

If a blocked nose is making you hesitate to use CPAP, check the mask and humidification in the order above first, and if it is still uncomfortable, have your nose checked.
