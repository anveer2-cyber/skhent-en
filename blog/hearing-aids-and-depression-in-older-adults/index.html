Hearing Loss and Depression in Older Adults: How Hearing Aids May Help

4 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: September 4, 2024 Read the original (한국어) ↗

Depression is one of the most common mental health problems worldwide, and it has many causes. Among them, a growing number of studies report a close link between hearing loss and depression. Hearing loss makes everyday communication harder and can lead to social isolation, which can take a real toll on mental health.

This article looks at how hearing loss and depression are connected, and how hearing aids can help.

How hearing loss affects mental health

Hearing loss is not simply a matter of not hearing sounds. It makes everyday conversation and social contact harder, and as a result people gradually come to feel isolated. Studies show that people with hearing loss are more likely to experience depression than people without it.

  • Less social activity. As conversation becomes harder, people tend to take part in social activities less. This brings a sense of isolation, which can lead to depression. This is seen especially often in older adults.
  • Misunderstandings and stress. With hearing loss, misunderstandings in everyday conversation become more common, and people often have to ask others to repeat themselves. Repeated over and over, this can cause friction in the family, add stress and make people avoid conversations.
  • Lower quality of life. Hearing loss can take away everyday pleasures and lower overall quality of life. If this continues, a sense of helplessness can grow and lead to depression.

The role of hearing aids

Hearing aids can improve communication and may ease social isolation and depressive symptoms in some people. They do not guarantee prevention of depression or replace mental health assessment and treatment. When hearing loss is compensated for, communication becomes easier, which helps reduce social isolation and stress.

  • Getting back to social life. When people can follow conversations more clearly, including in noisier places, their confidence in conversation returns. This may support social participation and wellbeing, although the benefit varies from person to person.
  • Easier conversations. People may need fewer repetitions and find conversation less tiring, although hearing aids do not restore normal hearing, particularly in background noise.
  • A better quality of life. With better hearing, people return to the world of sound: TV, radio and conversation. As activities that had dropped away because of hearing loss come back, feelings of helplessness and low mood are more likely to improve.

What the research says

Several studies have looked at the link between hearing loss and depression. They report that depression is more common in people with hearing loss.

A 2014 cross-sectional analysis of 18,318 US adults found an association between hearing loss and depressive symptoms. In a subgroup of women aged 70 and over, audiometrically measured moderate hearing loss was also associated with depressive symptoms (JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery). This was not a trial of hearing aids.

Research includes both observational studies and randomised trials. A 2025 community-based trial in 221 older adults in China found improved depressive-symptom scores after 6 months of hearing-aid support compared with a waiting-list group. The trial was unblinded and its findings may not apply to everyone. This is not proof that hearing aids alone prevent or cure clinical depression.

Why early management of hearing loss matters

Finding hearing loss early, and using hearing support such as hearing aids when needed, is important. If hearing loss is suspected, a hearing test with an ENT doctor is the first step, followed by considering a hearing aid if appropriate.

Hearing loss often progresses slowly, so people may not notice it themselves. Regular hearing tests help you know where your hearing stands and act when there is a problem.

Supporting a parent through hearing rehabilitation

When hearing aids improve communication, they can help with social activity and quality of life, and some studies have also reported an association with fewer depressive symptoms. If depressive symptoms are clear, please also see a mental health specialist. If hearing loss is suspected, it's worth getting professional help and a properly prescribed hearing aid, to improve quality of life and protect mental health. Family support also makes a big difference to whether an older person keeps using a hearing aid; see why some people give up on their hearing aids.

Low mood needs its own attentionHearing aids are not a substitute for depression care. Persistent low mood or loss of interest needs assessment by a doctor or mental health professional. If someone has seriously harmed themselves or may act on thoughts of self-harm now, call the local emergency number (119 in Korea) or go to an emergency department immediately; do not wait for a routine appointment.
