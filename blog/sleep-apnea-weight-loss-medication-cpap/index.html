Sleep & Snoring

Sleep Apnea and Weight-Loss Medication: Do You Still Need Your CPAP?

9 min read Written by Dr. Jungho Oh, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: September 15, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

"If I lose weight with an injection, will my snoring get better?"

"I have sleep apnea — could a weight-loss medication help me?"

"Once the weight comes off, can I stop using my CPAP?"

These are questions I hear more and more from patients who come to our clinic with snoring and sleep apnea. "Lose weight" used to be general advice. Now there are prescription medications that produce substantial weight loss, and one of them has been approved specifically for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

The short answer For adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, a weekly prescription weight-management injection (a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist) can be one treatment option — in Korea it was approved for this use in August 2025. But it is not a treatment for snoring alone. Tirzepatide may improve OSA in eligible adults with obesity, but starting it is not a reason to stop prescribed CPAP on your own. Treatment changes require clinical review and, when appropriate, objective reassessment of OSA.

Below I separate what has been shown in research from what is still only hoped for.

1. What kind of medication is this?

The medication discussed here is tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist. Its Korean OSA indication applies to adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA alongside diet and exercise. The evidence is specific to tirzepatide and the regimens studied; it should not be extrapolated to all medicines in this class or to arbitrary lower-dose regimens. Eligibility and dosing must be decided by the prescriber under the current Korean label.

It is a once-weekly injection that acts on two hormone receptors (GIP and GLP-1) involved in appetite and blood-sugar control. In Korea its approved uses are:

  • Blood-sugar control in adults with type 2 diabetes, alongside diet and exercise.
  • Weight management in adults with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27–30 with a weight-related condition, alongside diet and exercise.
  • Treatment of moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity (BMI 30 or more), alongside a reduced-calorie diet and exercise.

The U.S. FDA approved the sleep-apnea indication in December 2024; Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) approved it separately in August 2025. If you have read overseas news about "a weight-loss drug approved for sleep apnea," this is the same active ingredient — product names differ between countries, so please ask your doctor rather than relying on brand names.

2. Has it actually been shown to help sleep apnea?

Yes. The evidence comes from the SURMOUNT-OSA trials, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2024: two phase 3 trials in 469 adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA at 60 sites in 9 countries, followed for 52 weeks.

  • Trial 1 (234 people) included patients who were not using CPAP — because they could not tolerate it or did not want it.
  • Trial 2 (235 people) included patients who had already been on CPAP for at least 3 months.

The main outcome was the change in the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) — the number of times per hour your breathing stops or becomes shallow during sleep.

One number is often misquoted. In this study, 42.2% (Trial 1) and 50.2% (Trial 2) of participants met a pre-defined combined target ("AHI under 5, or AHI 5–14 with a daytime sleepiness score of 10 or less"). This is sometimes reported as "half of patients got rid of their sleep apnea." That is not what it means. It means they met the study's composite criterion — not that everyone's AHI became normal, and not that they could stop CPAP.

It is also important to know who was studied: people with fairly severe sleep apnea (average AHI around 50 per hour) and an average BMI of about 39. People with diabetes (type 1 or type 2) were excluded. So the results cannot simply be applied to mild sleep apnea, to people without obesity, or to people with diabetes.

3. Who is it approved for in Korea?

"As an adjunct to a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity, for the treatment of moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity and an initial BMI of 30 kg/m² or more."

That one sentence contains four conditions: adult; initial BMI 30 or above; sleep apnea of at least moderate severity; and used alongside — not instead of — diet and exercise. Loud snoring or being a little overweight is not, by itself, a reason to use it. Whether sleep apnea is "moderate or worse" is decided by the AHI measured on a sleep study, not by how it feels.

4. So can I stop using my CPAP?

This is the most important part of this article. Do not stop CPAP on your own because you have lost weight or started tirzepatide. SURMOUNT-OSA did not determine when CPAP can safely be discontinued. After objective reassessment, your sleep clinician may recommend continued CPAP, a pressure adjustment, or, in selected patients, supervised discontinuation. There are two reasons for caution.

The Trial 2 results were measured with CPAP switched off

Because Trial 2 enrolled CPAP users, it is tempting to read it as "adding the medication to CPAP lowered AHI by another 29 points." That is not the case. Participants were asked to stop CPAP for 7 days before each sleep study, so the effect of the medication could be measured on its own. The 29.3 figure was therefore measured after a week without CPAP. The trial was not designed to show how much better people do with both treatments together, or when it is safe to stop CPAP.

Korean guidelines treat weight management as a complement, not a substitute

The 2026 Korean Society of Sleep and Breathing guideline on obesity management in adult OSA states clearly that weight management complements CPAP, oral appliances and surgery — it does not replace them. It also sets out an order: diet and exercise first, and GLP-1–based medication considered when that is not enough. Starting with an injection is not the standard path. The guideline labels this combined statement optional. It rates evidence for reduced PAP needs or pressure and cardiometabolic benefits as Grade B, but evidence for improved short-term PAP adherence as Grade C; better nightly adherence should not be promised.

5. If I lose weight and snore less, am I in the clear?

Weight loss reduces fat around the neck and the base of the tongue, so the airway narrows less during sleep and snoring often becomes quieter. Many people then feel they are "fine now."

But snoring and apnea are not the same thing. The sound can decrease while the number of breathing pauses stays the same — and some people hardly snore at all yet have significant drops in oxygen. That is why you cannot judge this by how you feel.

Current evidence suggests the medication improves sleep apnea mainly indirectly, through weight loss. It has not been shown to open the airway directly; SURMOUNT-OSA compared tirzepatide with placebo; it was not a weight-loss-matched comparison with another intervention.

6. What is checked before this medication is considered?

First, the diagnosis. Sleep apnea treatment is not started on the basis of snoring or weight alone. A sleep study (polysomnography) to measure AHI and oxygen drops is the starting point. Since the approval is limited to "moderate-to-severe" OSA, it is impossible to know whether someone is even eligible without testing.

Second, as an ENT clinic we look at the airway itself. Sleep apnea is not only about weight. The blockage may be in the nose and nasopharynx, the tonsils and soft palate, the base of the tongue or the larynx — often several levels at once. If a deviated septum or enlarged turbinates make nasal breathing difficult, discomfort will remain with weight loss or CPAP unless that is addressed too. This is why we examine from the nose to the larynx with an endoscope.

Third, whether the medication is safe for you. The following must be checked first:

  • Personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma, or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2)
  • Type 1 diabetes
  • History of pancreatitis or gallbladder disease
  • Pregnancy, plans for pregnancy, or breastfeeding
  • Current diabetes medications — especially insulin or sulfonylureas

7. Side effects and when it must not be used

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal. In the trials, diarrhea occurred in 21–26%, nausea in 21–25% and vomiting in 9–17% of participants — mostly mild to moderate and more common while the dose was being increased.

Less common but important: two cases of acute pancreatitis occurred in the medication group of Trial 2 (there were no deaths). Gallstones and gallbladder inflammation, dehydration and kidney injury from vomiting or diarrhea, serious allergic reactions, and low blood sugar when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas also need to be watched for.

It must not be used if you have
  • A personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), or MEN2
  • A known hypersensitivity to the drug or its ingredients
  • Type 1 diabetes
  • Pregnancy — stop if you become pregnant or are planning pregnancy. It should also not be used while breastfeeding.

One practical point: this class of medication slows stomach emptying. If you are scheduled for surgery or a procedure under general anesthesia or deep sedation, or for an upper GI endoscopy, always tell the medical team that you are taking it.

8. When should I be re-tested?

Feeling better after weight loss is not a reason to stop follow-up. The 2026 Korean guideline gives this its strongest recommendation and specifies the timing:

  • Re-evaluate with a sleep study (in-lab or home) 6–12 months after weight loss.
  • Re-test whenever weight changes by 10% or more, or symptoms come back.
  • If you use CPAP, review the usage data and residual apnea every 3–6 months.

For people using PAP, the guideline also suggests repeat PSG or HSAT about 3–6 months after achieving at least 10% weight loss, or earlier if residual AHI, symptoms or mask leak become problematic, with further reassessment at 6–12 months. Your clinician should individualize the timing.

In other words, treatment is not finished when the weight comes off; it includes checking, with numbers, how much apnea remains at your new weight. Only then can we decide whether to adjust CPAP pressure, continue as before, or try reducing it.

Summary

  • In Korea, one prescription weight-management injection is approved for moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity (initial BMI 30 or more).
  • In a 52-week trial, AHI and body weight fell clearly more than with placebo — but the trial did not show a "cure."
  • SURMOUNT-OSA did not establish when an individual can safely discontinue CPAP. Continued PAP, pressure adjustment or supervised discontinuation should be considered after individualized reassessment.
  • Korean guidelines recommend diet and exercise first, with medication considered when that is not enough.
  • Gastrointestinal side effects are common, there are contraindications, and it must not be used in pregnancy or breastfeeding.
  • Even if you improve, a repeat sleep study 6–12 months later to measure remaining apnea is part of treatment.

Sleep apnea is not a noise problem; it is a problem of oxygen levels falling again and again while you sleep. Having another treatment option is good news, but who it suits — and how your current treatment should be adjusted — should be decided based on test results. If you are worried about your breathing during sleep, a sleep study is the right first step.
