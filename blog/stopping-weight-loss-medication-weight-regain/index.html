Stopping Weight-Loss Medication: Will the Weight Come Back?

9 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: September 2, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

"Can I lower my maintenance dose?"

"If I stop the injections, will the weight come back?"

"Do I have to stay on them for life once I reach my goal weight?"

These questions come up often from patients using prescription weight-management medications. In this article, "weight-management medication" means the weekly injectable GLP-1–based medications — either GLP-1 receptor agonists or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists — that are prescribed by a doctor for obesity.

When we review a patient on this kind of treatment, we don't only look at how many kilograms were lost. We also check whether appetite is stably controlled, whether muscle mass and daily habits are being maintained, and whether weight-related conditions such as snoring and sleep apnea are improving.

The short answer Not everyone needs the same medication at the same dose for life. But on average, the research shows that many people regain appetite and weight after stopping. So reaching your goal weight is less "the end of treatment" and more the point at which you move from the weight-loss phase to a long-term maintenance phase.

Why does weight come back after stopping?

These medications do not simply make you eat less by upsetting your stomach. They act on appetite and fullness signals between the brain and the gut, helping to:

  • reduce hunger and "food noise"
  • make you feel full on smaller portions
  • reduce cravings for sweet and fatty food
  • cut down impulsive snacking and late-night eating

When the medication is stopped and its effect gradually wears off, the original appetite signals can return. This does not mean you were "addicted" or lacked willpower. It reflects the fact that obesity, like high blood pressure or diabetes, is a chronic condition that tends to relapse.

What the trials show after stopping

Semaglutide (STEP 1 extension)

In the STEP 1 extension, adults with overweight or obesity and without diabetes had received semaglutide at the trial regimen or placebo. In the semaglutide arm of the 327-person extension analysis, mean weight loss was 17.3% at week 68; during the following year, participants regained 11.6 percentage points, or about two-thirds of the lost weight. Both study medication and the structured lifestyle intervention were stopped, so this was not an isolated test of medication withdrawal with the same lifestyle programme maintained. Blood pressure, blood sugar and other metabolic markers also tended to drift back toward pre-treatment levels.

Tirzepatide (SURMOUNT-4)

SURMOUNT-4 studied tirzepatide at the trial’s maximum tolerated maintenance regimen in adults with overweight or obesity and without diabetes. Of 783 people entering the lead-in phase, 670 were randomized after 36 weeks, by which time they had lost 20.9% on average. Over the next 52 weeks, weight changed by −5.5% with continued tirzepatide and +14.0% after switching to placebo, each relative to week-36 weight, while lifestyle counselling continued in both groups.

The proportion who kept at least 80% of their initial weight loss was 89.5% with continued treatment versus 16.6% after stopping.

Even so, "everyone regains everything" is not accurate either. In a post hoc subgroup of 308 participants who had lost at least 10% by week 36 and had a subsequent weight measurement, 17.5% regained less than one-quarter of the lost weight; approximately 4% continued to lose weight while lifestyle support continued. These selected subgroup results cannot predict an individual patient's outcome after stopping. However, there is currently no reliable way to predict, from tests or patient characteristics before treatment, who will maintain their weight without medication.

Is a lower maintenance dose enough?

SURMOUNT-MAINTAIN (2026) specifically studied tirzepatide. After a 60-week lead-in, eligible participants were randomized to continue the maximum tolerated maintenance regimen, reduce to one prespecified lower maintenance regimen, or switch to placebo for 52 weeks. Model-estimated weight change from the original baseline at week 112 was −21.9%, −16.6% and −9.9%, respectively. From week 84, rescue tirzepatide could be used if more than half of the initial weight loss was regained; rescue was used in approximately 8%, 25% and 67% of the groups. The primary analysis used a prespecified approach to account for rescue, rather than treating post-rescue improvements as benefits of the originally assigned regimen. These results do not establish an effective lower dose for other medicines or for OSA treatment.

So a lower dose can be a better maintenance option than stopping completely — but it was not as effective as staying on the higher dose: about 1 in 4 people on the lower dose still regained significant weight. The trial also only included adults with an average BMI of about 40, without diabetes, who could tolerate the higher doses, so its results should not be applied as a one-size-fits-all maintenance dose. What matters is not the lowest number, but the individually appropriate dose that maintains weight and health while keeping side effects and treatment burden as low as possible.

What about lowering the dose of other GLP-1 medications?

For GLP-1 receptor agonists, continuing treatment also helps maintain weight, and stopping tends to lead to regain. But dedicated long-term trials comparing a step-down to a specific lower dose are still limited, so the SURMOUNT-MAINTAIN results cannot simply be transferred. Whether to stay on your current dose or lower it within the approved range should be decided with your doctor, considering:

  • current weight and waist size
  • how much hunger and food noise you have
  • recent weight trend
  • blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and fatty liver
  • snoring and sleep apnea
  • side effects such as nausea or constipation
  • cost and whether you can sustain the treatment long term

Does tapering slowly prevent rebound?

There is not yet good-quality evidence that gradually reducing the dose prevents weight regain better than stopping abruptly. In practice, doctors sometimes step the dose down under supervision to find the lowest effective dose and watch appetite and weight. This is not a proven method for preventing rebound, and it is different from changing the schedule yourself. Stretching the interval between injections to 10 days or 2 weeks on your own, or splitting doses from a higher-dose pen, is not an approved way to use these medications and makes the actual dose, hygiene and safety uncertain.

So do I need it for life?

The WHO describes obesity as a chronic, relapsing disease and has issued a conditional recommendation that GLP-1–based medicines can be used as long-term treatment. But long-term treatment does not mean everyone stays on the same medication at the same dose forever. Depending on the person, three strategies can be considered:

  1. Continue an effective dose — e.g. if appetite and food noise return quickly when a dose is delayed, if you have had repeated large rebounds in the past, or if you have weight-related conditions (severe obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, fatty liver, sleep apnea) where the health benefits also need to be weighed.
  2. Lower the dose with your doctor — if your goal weight has been stable for some time and appetite and eating patterns are well controlled. Afterwards, monitor waist size, hunger, snacking and metabolic markers as well as weight.
  3. Stop with a plan — because of cost, side effects, pregnancy plans, a new health problem or personal choice. The key is to agree in advance when to come back and what to do if appetite and weight start to rise.

Five principles for keeping the weight off

1. Set a weight range, not a single number

Nobody stays at exactly the same weight every day. Agree on an acceptable range around your goal and a "warning line" at which you'll check in again. Track waist size, blood pressure, blood sugar, fatty liver and how you feel day to day, not just the scale.

2. Watch for appetite signals that come back before the weight does

  • thinking about food much of the day
  • larger portions
  • more frequent snacks or late-night eating
  • harder to control appetite as the next injection day approaches

These are not signs of weak willpower; they are signals that the intensity of treatment needs to be reassessed.

3. Prioritize protein when you're eating less

When portions shrink, it is easy to eat mostly salad and fall short on protein. Put easy-to-eat protein first — fish, lean meat, eggs, tofu, beans, dairy. Vegetables can be cooked or finely chopped rather than eaten in large raw portions. If you have kidney or liver disease, protein intake should be adjusted individually.

4. Add strength training to aerobic exercise

Rapid weight loss can include some loss of lean mass, not just fat. Adequate protein plus strength training 2–3 times a week helps maintain strength and physical function. Start with something sustainable: standing up from a chair, resistance bands, light squats.

5. Aim for sustainable change, not a perfect diet

If someone who used to eat four ice creams can now stop at one, that is real change. Rather than banning all eating out and snacks at once, start with high-impact, easy-to-change habits: sugary drinks, frequent late-night eating, and habitual ultra-processed food.

Do stronger side effects mean it's working better?

No. Studies of both medication types found that gastrointestinal side effects such as nausea, vomiting and diarrhea explain only a small part of the overall weight loss. You do not need to be unable to eat or be sick for the medication to work. If side effects are troublesome, talk to your doctor about adjusting the pace of dose increases or the dose itself rather than pushing through.

If you snore or have sleep apnea, check one more thing

Obesity and obstructive sleep apnea are closely linked. In the SURMOUNT-OSA trial, treatment with the dual GIP/GLP-1 medication in people with obesity and moderate-or-worse sleep apnea reduced not only weight but also the apnea–hypopnea index and the overnight oxygen deficit.

However, losing weight is not a reason to stop CPAP on your own. Snoring may get quieter while sleep apnea remains. If your symptoms or weight have changed significantly, it is safer to re-evaluate with a sleep study — both whether treatment is still needed and whether CPAP pressure should be adjusted.

That is why, for patients who snore or have sleep apnea, we look beyond the scale: daytime sleepiness, sleep quality, CPAP use and, where needed, sleep study results.

Summary

  • Not everyone regains weight in the same way after stopping weight-management medication.
  • But on average, regain after stopping is a common outcome, not a rare exception.
  • Once you reach your goal, decide with your doctor whether to (1) continue an effective dose, (2) lower the dose and monitor, or (3) stop — and if so, what to monitor and for how long.

Success in weight management isn't seeing your lowest number on the scale once. The real goal is a healthier body and lifestyle that you can sustain comfortably over time.
