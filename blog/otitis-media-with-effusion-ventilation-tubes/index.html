Blocked Ear? Otitis Media with Effusion (Glue Ear) and Ventilation Tubes

4 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: March 9, 2020 Read the original (한국어) ↗

Some people come in saying their ear feels blocked and they can't hear well. The first concern is sudden hearing loss, but when the endoscope shows fluid behind the eardrum, the cause is often otitis media with effusion (sometimes called glue ear). In children, it is also often found when the ears are checked during a visit for a cold or sore throat.

What are the symptoms?

Unlike acute otitis media, OME usually causes fullness and reduced hearing rather than marked ear pain or fever, although mild discomfort can occur. A clear, yellowish fluid (effusion) collects in the middle ear, the space behind the eardrum.

Bacteria can sometimes grow in this fluid and turn it into acute otitis media, which does cause ear pain and fever.

What causes it?

Most cases come from poor Eustachian tube function. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the nasopharynx, the space at the very back of the nose, and keeps the pressure in the middle ear balanced. If the tube doesn't work well, negative pressure builds up in the middle ear and fluid collects there. Children's Eustachian tubes work less well than adults', so otitis media with effusion is more common in children.

How is it diagnosed?

An endoscope usually makes the diagnosis easy: there is yellow, amber-coloured fluid or there are air bubbles behind the eardrum. But many cases are less clear. Hearing tests then help:

  • Pure-tone audiometry may show a gap between bone-conduction and air-conduction thresholds (a conductive hearing loss).
  • Tympanometry may show that the eardrum moves poorly.

Telling it apart from other conditions

Sudden hearing loss and Ménière's disease can also cause ear fullness and reduced hearing, so they need to be distinguished from otitis media with effusion. In some people, ear wax packed tightly in the ear canal causes similar symptoms. An accurate diagnosis is needed for the right treatment.

Don't wait ifHearing drops suddenly in one ear, especially without a cold or blocked nose, or comes with dizziness. Sudden hearing loss needs prompt assessment. Ear pain with fever may mean an acute infection and should also be checked.

How is it treated?

Watchful waiting first

Many children without developmental risk can be monitored initially, because OME often clears over about 3 months. Hearing should be assessed as part of the evaluation and before tube surgery is considered. Earlier review may be needed if hearing difficulties substantially affect daily life or the child is at risk of speech, language or learning problems. Steroids, antihistamines, decongestants and antibiotics do not work for the fluid itself and are not recommended. Related conditions such as allergic rhinitis are treated separately.

For children who can perform the technique, a clinician may recommend autoinflation, for example with an appropriate nasal balloon device. It may help fluid clear in the short term, but its effect on hearing is uncertain and it can cause ear pain. Use the technique as instructed and stop if it hurts; do not force pressure into the ears.

Ventilation tube insertion

If fluid persists, ventilation tube (grommet) insertion is considered according to hearing results, the effect on daily life and individual risk, rather than the passage of 3 months alone. In adults this can be done as a simple outpatient procedure under local anaesthesia with an endoscope; children usually need a general anaesthetic:

  1. A local anaesthetic is applied to the eardrum and left for about 10 minutes.
  2. Under the endoscope, a small cut is made in the eardrum.
  3. The fluid in the middle ear is drawn out with suction.
  4. A very small ventilation tube is placed in the opening.

Tubes can improve hearing and the blocked feeling caused by persistent middle-ear fluid, although the result varies. Possible risks include ear discharge or infection, eardrum scarring and a hole that remains after the tube comes out. Follow-up is needed to check hearing, the eardrum and the tube.

In children, a ventilation tube is considered when the fluid has lasted 3 months or more with hearing loss or problems in daily life; children at risk of speech and language delay are assessed earlier. In adults the decision is made individually, depending on the symptoms and the cause. Persistent or recurrent fluid in just one ear in an adult needs assessment of the underlying cause, including the nasopharynx and Eustachian tube opening. Further tests are chosen from the examination findings; inserting a tube does not replace that evaluation. The aim is to offer the treatment that best fits each patient's needs.
