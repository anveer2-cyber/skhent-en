Dizziness & Balance

Atypical BPPV: Short-Arm Variants and Persistent Nystagmus — A Clinical Reference

6 min read Written by Dr. Jungho Oh, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: June 3, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

This is a clinical reference for BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo) that does not respond well to standard repositioning — especially short-arm BPPV. It is written mainly for clinicians and for patients who want the technical detail; if you simply have positional dizziness, start with BPPV basics and home exercises and see a doctor.

ScopeMost techniques for short-arm BPPV are supported only by small studies. They are used as a second step after standard manoeuvres fail, and if there are signs suggesting a central cause, referral to neurology for central vertigo comes first.

When to suspect atypical BPPV

Suspect it when the Dix–Hallpike or roll test shows atypical nystagmus (absent, opposite direction, no latency, non-fatiguing, persistent), or when symptoms recur or persist immediately after repositioning. Classify by ① nystagmus pattern (geotropic/apogeotropic, upbeat/downbeat), ② persistent vs transient, and ③ whether conversion occurs.

Where the otoconia sit decides both the nystagmus pattern and the choice of treatment. They can be free-floating in the long arm of the posterior canal (canalithiasis), attached to the cupula (cupulolithiasis), or trapped in the short arm of the posterior canal. (The anatomical illustrations in the Korean original are third-party figures and are not reproduced here.)

A. Short-arm lateral (horizontal) canal BPPV

Treated the same way as apogeotropic horizontal canal BPPV (HC-BPPV). There is no dedicated protocol for horizontal short-arm BPPV; it is managed as a form of persistent apogeotropic HC-BPPV. The only practical difference from cupulolithiasis is how easily it converts.

Diagnosis

  • First separate geotropic vs apogeotropic and persistent vs transient with the supine roll test.
  • Persistent apogeotropic = heavy cupula (including cupulolithiasis) · persistent geotropic = light cupula · transient apogeotropic = canalithiasis on the short-arm side · transient geotropic = canalithiasis on the long-arm side.
  • Horizontal short-arm canalithiasis appears apogeotropic and is easily confused with cupulolithiasis/heavy cupula (initial nystagmus alone can't confirm it). Some models try to separate short-arm (more asymmetric/unilateral apogeotropic with the affected side down) from cupulolithiasis (bilateral persistent apogeotropic), but the literature is divided → the most reliable clue is conversion (below).
  • If persistent geotropic, consider light cupula before short-arm.
  • Affected side — geotropic: the stronger side is affected; apogeotropic: the weaker side is affected (pseudo-spontaneous nystagmus beats toward the healthy side).
  • Bow and Lean test (Choung 2006): geotropic = bow → affected side, lean → healthy side; apogeotropic = lean → affected side, bow → healthy side. (Direction rules are error-prone, so judge together with roll-test intensity and pseudo-spontaneous nystagmus.)

The key to differentiation: conversion

If head-shaking, forced prolonged positioning (FPP) or repeated roll tests convert apogeotropic to geotropic nystagmus, the cause is presumed to be canalithiasis (including short-arm); if it doesn't convert, attached cupulolithiasis is presumed. A change in intensity (crescendo) is not a validated criterion.

Treatment

Principle: there is no dedicated horizontal short-arm manoeuvre → treat as apogeotropic HC-BPPV, in the order induce conversion → remove.

  1. Induce conversion: head-shaking / forced prolonged positioning / repeated supine roll.
  2. Remove: once converted to geotropic — Gufoni (healthy side down, nose down), barbecue (Lempert) roll, FPP. If persistently apogeotropic — Gufoni-Appiani (affected side down, nose up), Zuma e Maia, modified Gufoni, Vannucchi-Asprella.

The Gufoni direction differs by type — it is not "always toward the healthy side."

Data: success rates vary widely by study and by how cases are defined (roughly 20–70%+ for a single session). One study reported single-session resolution of 62–76% with a modified Gufoni manoeuvre for HC-BPPV cupulolithiasis, and in patients with prior recurrences Zuma was reported to outperform Appiani. Interpret with repetition, natural history and conversion in mind.

Mastoid vibration is an adjunct only (no evidence of routine benefit).

B. Short-arm posterior canal BPPV

Unlike the horizontal canal, treatment differs. This is the typical variant in which the standard Epley fails for structural reasons. The otoconia are either shaken out into the utricle (Bow-and-Yaw) or converted into the long arm and then cleared with an Epley.

Diagnosis

  • Atypical presentation: weak or no nystagmus/vertigo on the standard Dix–Hallpike, but vertigo or backward trunk pull (retropulsion) when sitting up.
  • (Augmented) half Dix–Hallpike: lie back only 45° without letting the head hang → persistent upbeat torsional nystagmus (sustained UBTN) lasting over 60 seconds with no latency strongly suggests short-arm canalithiasis or cupulolithiasis.
  • The inversion test (behaviour on sitting up) helps distinguish short-arm canalithiasis (free otoconia) from attached cupulolithiasis/heavy cupula.

Why the Epley fails

The Epley assumes the otoconia are in the long arm. If they're trapped in the short arm, they are blocked by the cupula and spin in place, unable to pass into the long arm or drain into the utricle. → A short-arm manoeuvre or conversion into the long arm is essential.

Treatment

① Bow-and-Yaw manoeuvre (Ping et al.) — stand-alone first-line

  1. Have the patient sit upright or kneel.
  2. Bow the head deeply forward, about 135° (bow — top of the head toward the floor).
  3. In that position, rapidly turn the head 45° left and right alternately (yaw = head-shaking while bowed).
  4. Re-test with the Dix–Hallpike — if negative, this both confirms the short-arm type and completes treatment.

Both kneeling and seated versions work on the same principle; use whichever is comfortable for the patient.

② Bascule manoeuvre (Castellucci et al. 2025) — case-based, 2-step (conversion then Epley); for experienced clinicians

  1. Turn the head 45° toward the healthy side.
  2. Quickly lay the patient on their side, nose down (a demi-Semont position).
  3. Quickly rotate head and trunk 180° to the affected side, nose up.
  4. Quickly reverse 180° back to the healthy side, nose down — repeat this side-to-side roll 5 times in each direction.
  5. Once converted to the typical (long-arm) form, finish with a standard Epley.

The axis of movement is a side-to-side roll (not sitting up and lying down). In this manoeuvre the otoconia move step by step from the short arm toward the opening of the common crus and then, via the common crus, to the lowest part of the long arm, from where a standard Epley can clear them.

③ Mastoid vibration — adjunct
Not a stand-alone treatment. A bone-conduction vibrator is applied to the mastoid on the affected side during the above manoeuvres to help free attached or resistant otoconia (Taura reported Epley non-responders who responded to vibration).

Yacovino (deep head hanging) — note the indication
The proven indication is anterior canal BPPV (downbeat nystagmus). Short-arm posterior canal BPPV usually shows upbeat nystagmus, so it is unsuitable. ⚠ Downbeat nystagmus is itself a central red flag, so "downbeat = Yacovino" is wrong: first exclude a central (e.g. cerebellar) cause — weighing pure downbeat, repeatability, neurological signs and fixation suppression — and perform it only when a peripheral anterior canal cause is judged likely. Technique: sitting → lie back with the head hanging 30° below horizontal (extension) → chin to chest (flexion) → sit up. (The order is extend, then flex. Beware canal switch into the posterior canal.)

Central causes — red flags

  • Direction-fixed persistent nystagmus — the direction doesn't change when the head is turned left or right (in typical BPPV it changes). It may be a canalith jam (peripheral), but it is the hardest to tell apart from a central (cerebellar/nodular) cause.
  • Persistent (>1 minute), no latency or fatigue, no response to repeated repositioning.
  • Gaze-evoked nystagmus, skew deviation, cerebellar signs (e.g. marked gait ataxia), failure of fixation suppression. (A normal head impulse test is a marker in acute continuous vertigo [HINTS], not a stand-alone red flag for positional vertigo.)
  • Non-fatiguing pure downbeat nystagmus.

→ If these are present, consider neurology referral. (Apogeotropic nystagmus itself is not a central sign — cupulolithiasis, a common benign finding, is also apogeotropic.)

Summary

  • Horizontal short arm: no separate manoeuvre → treat as apogeotropic HC-BPPV. The only difference is how easily it converts.
  • Posterior short arm (type 2): Epley-resistant → Bow-and-Yaw (alone), or Bascule to convert to the long arm followed by Epley (with vibration if needed).
