Sleeping Worse After Taking an Antihistamine? Why It Happens and What to Do

3 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: September 16, 2025 Read the original (한국어) ↗

Here are some thoughts on a common concern in treating allergic rhinitis and hives (urticaria).

A restless night at home

A few days ago, a family member of mine whose hives had flared up from a house dust mite allergy took a levocetirizine antihistamine before bed. She already had mild insomnia, but that night she woke easily at every small movement of our child and felt she hadn't slept deeply at all by morning. A poor night's sleep makes everyone more irritable the next day.

That raised a question: "Why would someone sleep worse on a second-generation antihistamine?" — which is why I wrote this article.

Aren't antihistamines supposed to make you sleepy?

Antihistamines, often called "runny-nose medicine" or "skin medicine," are generally known to cause drowsiness.

Histamine (acting on H1 receptors) is an important neurotransmitter for keeping the brain awake. The antihistamines we take act in the skin and nasal lining to reduce histamine's effects, relieving itching and runny nose.

But when an antihistamine crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and acts on the brain, alertness drops and drowsiness can appear.

Antihistamines that cross the blood–brain barrier

For this reason doctors generally prescribe second-generation antihistamines first (for example epinastine, levocetirizine or fexofenadine). Because a first-generation antihistamine is sometimes more effective for symptom relief, a first-generation drug such as chlorpheniramine may still be chosen, with care about side effects.

  • First-generation antihistamines (chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, etc.) cross the blood–brain barrier easily and cause strong drowsiness and sedation.
  • Second-generation antihistamines (levocetirizine, epinastine, fexofenadine, etc.) penetrate the brain less, so they are relatively less sedating — but individual differences mean they can still affect sleep structure.

Why might sleep quality fall on a second-generation antihistamine?

Possible sleep fragmentation after levocetirizine

Levocetirizine is known not to cross the blood–brain barrier easily, so it's generally said to have little effect on sleep. But people differ in their sensitivity to medicines, and some experience sleep-related side effects.

Levocetirizine can have an initial calming effect that helps you fall asleep. In some people, however, sleep fragmentation can occur:

  • waking often during the night, or REM (dreaming) sleep being suppressed so sleep feels less restorative
  • as a result, the feeling that "I slept all night but don't feel refreshed at all"

What is sleep fragmentation?

Simply put, it's when sleep is "broken into pieces."

  • Deep sleep (slow-wave sleep, N3) and REM sleep decrease, and brief awakenings or light sleep repeat.
  • This can lead to daytime fatigue, poor concentration and mood changes.

For more on sleep stages, see how much deep and REM sleep is normal. If unrefreshing sleep continues even without medication, especially with snoring, consider checking for sleep apnea.

What to do if your sleep suffers on an antihistamine

  1. Change the timing — switching from bedtime to a morning dose often eases sleep fragmentation.
  2. Consider a different medicine — if symptoms are not severe, switching to an even less sedating antihistamine such as epinastine, fexofenadine or bilastine may help. Discuss this with your doctor or pharmacist rather than changing prescription medicines on your own.
  3. Improve sleep hygiene — avoid caffeine and alcohol, cut down on phones and bright light before bed, and make your bedroom comfortable.

Nasal steroid sprays are another option for allergic rhinitis that don't act on the brain in the same way — see the nasal spray guide.
