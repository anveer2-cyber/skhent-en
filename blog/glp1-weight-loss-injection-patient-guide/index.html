Weekly Weight-Loss Injections (GLP-1 Medications): A Patient Guide to Side Effects, Diet and Safety

10 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: June 2, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

From injection technique to managing side effects and planning meals — here is what people starting a weekly weight-management injection should know.

This article is for general information. Whether a medication is prescribed, and any dose changes or stopping, must be decided with your own doctor; what applies to you depends on your health.

1. What kind of medication is this?

This guide concerns prescription weekly semaglutide and tirzepatide injections. For adult weight management in Korea, the relevant product indications include an initial BMI of at least 30, or at least 27 with a weight-related condition, as an adjunct to diet and physical activity. Eligibility and other indications are product-specific and require medical assessment. Two active ingredients are covered:

  • Semaglutide — a GLP-1 receptor agonist
  • Tirzepatide — a dual agonist acting on both GIP and GLP-1 receptors

Both act on the brain's satiety centre to reduce appetite, and slow how fast the stomach empties, so you feel full for longer on less food. This delayed stomach emptying is central to how they work — and also the cause of the digestive side effects described below.

Before surgery or sedationTell your surgeon, endoscopy team and anaesthetist that you use this medicine before any procedure with general anaesthesia or deep sedation. Delayed stomach emptying can increase aspiration risk even after fasting. Do not stop or alter treatment yourself; the team will decide on an individual plan.
The most important premiseThe medication is a support, not magic. It helps hold down appetite, but it does not replace eating habits and exercise. Obesity should be approached as a chronic condition managed long-term, like high blood pressure or diabetes.

2. How do the two ingredients differ?

They belong to the same family but differ a little in how they act and in the evidence behind them. Which one suits you is decided with your doctor based on weight, other conditions and tolerability.

ItemSemaglutideTirzepatide
MechanismGLP-1 onlyGIP + GLP-1 (dual)
How it's givenOnce-weekly injection under the skinOnce-weekly injection under the skin
Average weight loss in clinical trialsAbout 15% at 68 weeks (STEP 1)About 15–21% at 72 weeks (SURMOUNT-1)
Oral contraceptive pillLittle effectTirzepatide may reduce the effectiveness of oral hormonal contraception. Use a non-oral method or add a barrier method for 4 weeks after starting and for 4 weeks after each dose increase. If vomiting or severe diarrhoea occurs, follow your contraceptive's instructions and seek advice.

In separate placebo-controlled trials in adults with overweight or obesity without diabetes, mean weight loss was about 15% at 68 weeks with semaglutide (STEP 1), and about 15–21% at 72 weeks across the tirzepatide groups (SURMOUNT-1), alongside lifestyle intervention. These are not head-to-head results or predictions for an individual.

Trial averages don't predict any one person's result; responses vary widely.

3. How the injection is given

  • Fixed day — inject once a week on the same day.
  • Site — abdomen, front of the thigh, or back of the upper arm. Move the spot a little each time rather than using the same place, which helps prevent lumps and uneven absorption.
  • Storage — keep refrigerated as a rule.
  • If you miss a dose — what to do depends on how many days remain until the next scheduled dose, so never inject twice on your own; follow the instructions you were given.
  • Needle disposal — used needles go in a dedicated sharps container, not ordinary rubbish.

4. The dose is increased slowly

Follow the prescribed schedule. Dose changes are individualized to response and tolerability; increasing faster than prescribed can worsen adverse effects. That is why treatment starts at the lowest dose and is stepped up gradually, at intervals your doctor sets, while watching how your body adapts.

Myth check"The faster you increase the dose, the more you lose" is not true. Higher doses do tend to give more weight loss on average, but forcing increases raises the risk of nausea, vomiting and delayed stomach emptying much more quickly. Meaningful weight loss is possible on a lower dose, so the right approach is to find, with your doctor, the maintenance dose that works for you without troublesome side effects. If side effects are bad, increases can be slowed.

5. Side effects and what to do

Common and usually temporary

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, bloating and indigestion mostly appear when starting or after a dose increase, and ease as the body adapts. Because the stomach empties slowly, overeating can make you vomit.

  • Cut meals to half to two-thirds of your usual amount; eat small amounts often, slowly, chewing well.
  • Avoid greasy or spicy food and fizzy drinks, which worsen nausea.

Muscle loss and hair loss

When weight falls quickly, you lose not only fat but also lean mass such as muscle and bone, and temporary hair shedding (telogen effluvium) can occur. Hair loss usually recovers with time, and muscle loss can be reduced a lot with the diet and exercise below.

  • Get enough protein at every meal, and do strength exercise 2–3 times a week.
  • Drink enough water (1.5–2 L a day) to prevent dehydration and constipation.
Seek care promptly if you have
  • Stop the medicine and seek urgent care for severe, persistent abdominal pain, which may spread to the back, with or without nausea or vomiting — possible pancreatitis. Call emergency services for breathing difficulty, collapse, or swelling of the tongue or throat.
  • Pain in the right upper abdomen or pit of the stomach (especially after fatty meals), fever, or yellowing of the skin/eyes — possible gallbladder disease
  • Cold sweats, shaking, dizziness (low blood sugar) — especially if you also take diabetes medicines
  • Vomiting or diarrhoea so severe that you pass less urine and may be dehydrated — rehydrate and get checked to protect the kidneys

6. Can pancreatitis and gallstones be prevented?

The two conditions differ in how likely they are.

Pancreatitis — the risk is lower than many think

Taken together, recent studies suggest these medications do not clearly raise the risk of acute pancreatitis. It isn't zero, though. No specific preventive medicine is needed; the key points are:

  • Follow the prescribed schedule — Acute pancreatitis is an uncommon but important safety concern. Follow the prescribed schedule for tolerability, but do not assume that gradual dose changes prevent pancreatitis. Stop the medicine and seek urgent medical assessment if pancreatitis is suspected.
  • Manage risk factors — limit alcohol, control triglycerides and gallstones.
  • Recognise it early — if the warning signs above appear, stop and get checked straight away.

Gallstones and cholecystitis — this deserves more attention

Gallstone risk rises for two overlapping reasons: ① the medication itself reduces gallbladder contraction and emptying, so bile stagnates; and ② rapid weight loss over-saturates bile with cholesterol.

Key points for preventing gallstones
  • Gallbladder problems can occur during treatment, and rapid weight loss is a risk factor. Aim for balanced, adequate nutrition and avoid crash diets. No particular serving of oil, nuts or avocado has been established as protection against medication-associated gallstones. Preventive medicines are considered only in selected clinical settings.
  • Don't cut out fat completely — the gallbladder needs a moderate amount of fat to contract and empty regularly (see the diet section).
  • Eat regular meals — avoid long fasts that let bile pool.
  • A gallstone-preventing medicine (ursodeoxycholic acid, UDCA) may be prescribed selectively by a doctor in high-risk situations such as rapid weight loss. Not every patient needs it.

7. What about blood tests?

Blood tests aren't mandatory, but for safety a baseline check before starting and periodic follow-up are recommended.

Baseline before starting

  • Kidney and liver function, electrolytes, fasting glucose, HbA1c
  • Lipids (especially triglycerides), thyroid function (TSH), complete blood count

Follow-up

Usually every 3–6 months, with longer intervals once the dose is stable. People with diabetes need closer checks of glucose and HbA1c, and kidney function deserves more attention if vomiting or diarrhoea were severe.

NoteAmylase and lipase may be checked when pancreatitis is suspected; enzyme elevation alone does not establish the diagnosis. Calcitonin is a separate test related to thyroid C-cell disease, not pancreatitis. Routine calcitonin screening has uncertain value, but a thyroid nodule or a significantly elevated result requires evaluation.

8. Diet guide — "what you eat decides whether the weight comes back"

While the medication holds your appetite down, use the chance to train yourself to fill up on nutritious food. Priorities: protein → complex carbohydrates → healthy fats.

Priority 1 · Protein first

Preventing muscle loss while losing weight keeps your basal metabolic rate up and makes regain less likely when the medication is reduced or stopped. Include chicken breast, fish, tofu or eggs at every meal.

Practical tipIf meat is hard to get down: to get 20 g of protein from eggs you need 3–4 eggs. If your stomach can't hold that, 2 eggs + half a block of tofu, or one protein shake (about 20 g), is easier on the stomach.

Priority 2 · Complex carbohydrates (about 30–40 g per meal)

Lots of carbohydrate makes you feel bloated, so think of about half your usual portion. Swap white rice, white bread and sugary drinks for fibre-rich whole grains.

  • Brown or multigrain rice, half a bowl (100 g) — about half a usual bowl of rice
  • One medium steamed sweet potato (100 g) — a little smaller than your fist
  • Oatmeal, 4–5 spoonfuls (40 g) — soaked in water or lactose-free milk

Priority 3 · Healthy fats

To help the gallbladder empty, as mentioned above, don't avoid fat altogether — add one of these each day. (This doesn't mean increasing total daily fat; it means including good fats so bile drains regularly.)

  • Olive oil, 1 spoonful — about 85% unsaturated fat (mainly monounsaturated); drizzle on salad
  • Roasted almonds, a handful (10–12)
  • Avocado, half — on a rice bowl or salad
Sample mealsBreakfast (light) — 40 g oatmeal + 1 protein shake + a handful of almonds
Lunch and dinner (filling) — half a bowl of brown rice + grilled chicken breast or a piece of salmon + vegetable salad (1 spoonful olive oil). On days you eat oily fish like salmon, you can skip the olive oil.

These amounts are examples. What you need depends on body size, sex, age and whether you have diabetes. Older adults in particular need more protein (1.2–1.5 g per kg of body weight), and people with diabetes need their carbohydrate amounts adjusted separately — work this out with your doctor.

9. When these medications must not be used

Contraindications differ by product and country. Tell your prescriber about any personal or family history of MTC, MEN2, or serious medicine allergy before treatment. Korean tirzepatide labeling contraindicates use with a personal or family history of MTC, MEN2, or hypersensitivity to its ingredients; the Korean semaglutide contraindication list is not identical. A serious reaction to another GLP-1 medicine also needs specialist review.

Pregnancy and breastfeedingDo not use these medicines for weight loss during pregnancy. Discuss contraception and pregnancy plans before treatment. Semaglutide should be stopped at least 2 months before a planned pregnancy. The stopping interval for tirzepatide is different (at least 1 month in current UK labeling); confirm the current Korean product instructions with your prescriber. Breastfeeding advice is also product- and country-specific.

Use with caution: with a history of severe pancreatitis, or current severe gastroparesis (stomach paralysis), the risks are weighed carefully with your medical team before deciding.

10. Older adults and people with chronic conditions

Treatment is possible, and for some conditions may even be recommended. But the goal must be different from cosmetic dieting.

Age 65 and over — "function" over "weight"

In older age, loss of muscle (sarcopenia) is the biggest enemy. Losing weight without a plan can mean much of the lost weight is muscle and bone (this differs by medication), leading to falls and fractures. Both treatments can reduce lean mass as well as fat. A 2026 observational preprint reported greater relative lean-mass loss with tirzepatide among people with paired body-composition measurements, but it cannot establish causation or predict an individual's loss of muscle function. Monitor nutrition, strength and mobility, especially in older or frail adults.

  • Start low, go slow — dose steps are held for longer than usual and increased slowly.
  • More high-quality protein (1.2–1.5 g per kg of body weight) than younger people, plus strength exercise such as sit-to-stand from a chair or resistance-band work.
  • Thirst is blunted, so dehydration and orthostatic hypotension (dizziness on standing up) are more likely. These directly cause falls, so check fluids and blood pressure often.

Points by condition

  • Cardiovascular disease. In SELECT, semaglutide reduced major cardiovascular events from 8.0% to 6.5% over about 40 months in adults aged at least 45 with established cardiovascular disease, BMI at least 27, and no diabetes (20% relative reduction). In SURPASS-CVOT, tirzepatide was noninferior, but not superior, to dulaglutide for major cardiovascular events in people with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. These results do not establish the same benefit for everyone seeking weight loss.
  • Type 2 diabetes. Insulin or sulfonylurea treatment may need adjustment to reduce hypoglycaemia risk. Ask your clinician for a glucose-monitoring and low-blood-sugar action plan; do not change doses yourself.
  • Reduced kidney or liver function. Suitability in kidney or liver disease depends on the product and severity. Korean semaglutide labeling does not recommend use in severe renal or severe hepatic impairment; tirzepatide has different instructions. Persistent vomiting or diarrhoea can cause dehydration and kidney injury. Fluid and protein targets must be individualized if you have kidney disease, heart failure or fluid restrictions.
The yardstickFor older adults and people with chronic conditions, judge progress less by the number on the scale and more by "are my body functions (grip strength, walking speed, electrolytes) being maintained?" — in close consultation with your doctor.

11. Results and long-term management

  • Results appear gradually, and there may be plateaus when weight stops falling for a while.
  • Weight regain is common after treatment stops. In the exploratory STEP 1 extension, participants regained about two-thirds of their previous weight loss in the year after semaglutide and structured lifestyle support were withdrawn. This is not a fixed prediction for every patient or every medicine. This isn't a lack of willpower: obesity is a chronic condition, and appetite and physiology rebound when the medication stops (see stopping weight-loss medication).
  • So approach it as long-term management, not a short-term diet, and plan any reduction or stopping together with your doctor.

12. Frequently asked questions

Will I lose more if I increase the dose quickly?

No. Higher doses give more weight loss on average, but forcing increases makes side effects such as nausea, vomiting and delayed stomach emptying rise far more steeply than the rate of weight loss. The right answer is to find, with your doctor, your own maintenance dose that works without troublesome side effects.

On the injection, can I eat anything and still lose weight?

You may lose weight early on because you eat less. But if you reach for sweets out of stress or habit rather than hunger, it is hard to get the full benefit. Changing your eating habits toward low-sugar, high-protein food at the same time is what makes treatment worthwhile.

Will the weight always come back when I stop?

Weight regain is common after treatment stops; the STEP 1 extension figure of about two-thirds is not a fixed prediction for every patient or every medicine. The medication works more like a "pacemaker" that holds appetite down, so it's important to change your eating habits while on it and to plan stopping step by step with your doctor.

Significant weight change can also change snoring and sleep apnea — if you use CPAP, don't stop it on your own; see sleep apnea and weight-loss medication.
