Dizziness & Balance

Brandt–Daroff Exercises for BPPV: Is It Safe to Do Them at Home?

5 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: June 4, 2026 Read the original (한국어) ↗

When I see patients with BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, often called "ear crystal" vertigo) in Jinhae and Changwon, I hear the same questions again and again:

"Can I treat BPPV myself?"

"Can I do Brandt–Daroff exercises at home?"

"Is it OK to follow a YouTube video?"

"If I push through the dizziness, will it get better faster?"

In short: Brandt–Daroff exercises can help, but they are not something every BPPV patient should do. And there is one more important fact, which I'll explain in the middle of this article.

What are Brandt–Daroff exercises?

BPPV happens when tiny crystal fragments (otoconia) break loose from the balance organ of the inner ear and drift into a semicircular canal, causing short, intense spinning whenever you change head position.

Brandt–Daroff exercises are a vestibular rehabilitation exercise: sitting on a bed, you lie down on one side, sit back up, then lie down on the other side, and repeat. By deliberately triggering dizziness with position changes, they help the brain get used to (habituate to) the signal and help disperse the crystal fragments. But this is definitely not an exercise to "just do whenever you feel dizzy."

BPPV has different types — and different treatments

Many people think of BPPV as one condition, but treatment depends on where the crystals are and in what state.

First, by which canal is involved:

  • Posterior canal BPPV (the most common)
  • Horizontal (lateral) canal BPPV
  • Anterior (superior) canal BPPV (rare)

And by the state of the crystals:

  • Canalithiasis — crystals floating freely in the canal
  • Cupulolithiasis — crystals stuck to the cupula (the sensor in the canal)

Because the right treatment position changes completely depending on the side, the canal and the type, we don't choose an exercise from the symptoms alone. We first find the exact location with nystagmus (eye-movement) testing and positional tests.

The most important fact: the first-line treatment is a repositioning manoeuvre, not exercise

This is the part many people don't know, and the point I most want to stress.

For the most common type, posterior canal BPPV, the first-line treatment is a canalith repositioning manoeuvre (such as the Epley manoeuvre), in which the doctor moves your head step by step to guide the crystals back where they belong. Studies report that the Epley manoeuvre has a higher single-session success rate than Brandt–Daroff exercises, and many patients improve markedly after one or two manoeuvres.

So rather than repeating Brandt–Daroff exercises at home for days, getting an accurate diagnosis and a repositioning manoeuvre at the clinic is often the quicker route. One accurate consultation can be more efficient than searching for exercises to do at home.

So when are Brandt–Daroff exercises used?

They still have a role, as a supplement, when:

  • You've been diagnosed with BPPV at a clinic,
  • the acute spinning has largely settled,
  • mild residual dizziness remains when you change position,
  • and your doctor has told you it's fine to do them at home.

They are also used when a repositioning manoeuvre is difficult to perform for some reason, or as a home maintenance exercise for people with frequent recurrences. In other words, Brandt–Daroff exercises are not a replacement for repositioning but a supplement and maintenance tool, done selectively after diagnosis.

Why can the wrong exercise be a problem?

With BPPV, the location of the crystals is everything. Repeating a position exercise that doesn't suit your type can:

  • make the dizziness worse
  • make nausea and vomiting worse
  • move the crystals into another canal (for example from the posterior to the horizontal canal, which makes treatment more complicated)
  • make it harder to tell which side was originally affected
  • delay the diagnosis of a dizziness disorder that isn't BPPV

Some people do come in after following videos and feeling worse, or confused about which side is the problem after trying several exercises at home. So "BPPV exercises" should not be applied to everyone in the same way.

How to do Brandt–Daroff exercises correctly

If you've been diagnosed and told this exercise is right for you, follow these steps:

  1. Sit upright on the edge of the bed.
  2. Turn your head about 45 degrees to one side.
  3. Keeping your head turned, lie down quickly on the opposite side. (Your nose will point up toward the ceiling.)
  4. Stay there until the dizziness settles, or for about 30 seconds.
  5. Slowly sit back up and wait about 30 seconds.
  6. Repeat the same movement in the other direction.

Both sides make one set. Usually 5 sets are done 2–3 times a day, continuing until you've had 2 days in a row without dizziness.

If you do them at home
  1. Do them on a bed. You can suddenly feel dizzy, so a bed is safer than the floor or a chair.
  2. Have someone with you at first. Older people or anyone at risk of falling shouldn't do them alone.
  3. Stop if the dizziness is severe. Forcing yourself through it can make things worse.
  4. See a doctor again if your symptoms change. For example, if you were dizzy only when lying on the right but now also on the left, or you stay dizzy even when not lying down, you need re-evaluation.

See a doctor instead of exercising if…

If any of the following appear, it may not be simple BPPV. Don't try the exercises — seek medical care right away:

  • Severe dizziness that continues even when you keep still
  • New hearing loss or loud tinnitus
When to seek emergency helpSudden speech difficulty, limb weakness, double vision, a new severe headache, or inability to walk safely may indicate stroke. Stop exercising and call emergency services immediately; do not wait for a clinic appointment. New sudden hearing loss also needs urgent medical assessment.

In these cases, other causes such as vestibular neuritis, Ménière's disease, sudden hearing loss and, rarely, stroke need to be ruled out.

In summary

Brandt–Daroff exercises can help people with BPPV. But not everyone needs to do them at home, and for the most common type, posterior canal BPPV, the first-line treatment is a repositioning manoeuvre in the clinic.

The key is the order: diagnosis first, exercise second. Because BPPV is treated differently depending on its type and side, getting an accurate diagnosis and checking that a method suits you is the safest and quickest way.
