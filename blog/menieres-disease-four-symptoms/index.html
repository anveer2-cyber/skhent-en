Dizziness & Balance

Ménière's Disease: Vertigo, Tinnitus, Ear Fullness and Hearing Loss Together

3 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: June 22, 2021 Read the original (한국어) ↗

Ménière’s disease can cause recurring episodes over a long period and often needs ongoing management. Other conditions, including BPPV, vestibular neuritis and sudden sensorineural hearing loss, have different courses and do not always resolve within a few weeks. Sudden hearing loss needs immediate medical assessment. Because improving eating habits is an important part of easing symptoms, much of the conversation in clinic is about diet.

For a fuller explanation of causes and treatment, see the Ménière's disease patient guide.

The four symptoms: vertigo, tinnitus, ear fullness and hearing loss

People with Ménière's disease tend to have recurring episodes. Stress, the season, the weather and poor eating habits can bring on an attack in which symptoms get worse. Some people describe a constant feeling of floating on a cloud. When symptoms are severe, hearing can drop. Hearing can fluctuate and may gradually become permanently reduced over time, even with treatment. Tinnitus and ear fullness may come and go, or may persist.

What is thought to cause it

Ménière's disease is thought to arise from endolymphatic hydrops — an increase in the volume of fluid (endolymph) in the inner ear's cochlea.

It has to be told apart from other conditions

Ménière's disease can be confused with other conditions such as vestibular migraine, sudden hearing loss, vestibular neuritis and BPPV. There is no single diagnostic test. Diagnosis depends on the pattern and duration of repeated vertigo episodes, a hearing test and fluctuating ear symptoms, while checking that another disorder does not explain them better. In definite Ménière’s disease, typical vertigo episodes last 20 minutes to 12 hours and low- to mid-frequency sensorineural hearing loss is documented. In practice it is often first mistaken for vestibular neuritis or sudden hearing loss, and because its symptoms resemble vestibular migraine, the diagnosis is usually reached over several visits, through consultation and testing over time.

Urgent and emergency signsSudden hearing loss needs immediate medical assessment. If dizziness comes with sudden slurred speech, limb weakness, double vision, inability to walk or a sudden severe headache, call the local emergency number (119 in Korea) immediately. Do not assume it is a Ménière’s attack or wait for a clinic appointment.

The goals of treatment: control and prevention

The first goal is to reduce how severe and how frequent the vertigo attacks are. People with severe symptoms may need hospital care again and again, and daily life can become impossible. Medication and lifestyle changes can ease the symptoms. Treatment aims to control vertigo, support hearing and improve quality of life. However, current treatment cannot guarantee prevention of permanent hearing loss, and hearing can worsen despite appropriate care. Regular hearing tests and support for hearing difficulties remain important.

Changing the eating habits that make it worse

Salt, coffee (caffeine), chocolate, alcohol and smoking can make symptoms worse. Lifestyle advice to cut down on salty food, caffeine and alcohol is recommended. Dietary changes are commonly discussed, but evidence of symptom benefit is limited and individual triggers vary. Agree any sodium target with your clinician rather than following a fixed spoonful limit. Soups and broths usually contain a lot of salt, so it helps to leave the broth.

Ménière's disease is hard to cure completely, but with good treatment it is a controllable form of dizziness. Medication and better eating habits allow most people to lead a normal daily life, so it is important to plan treatment together with a specialist.
