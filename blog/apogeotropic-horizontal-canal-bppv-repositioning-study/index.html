Dizziness & Balance

Paper Review: Treating Apogeotropic Horizontal Canal BPPV (Cupulolithiasis) — Two Manoeuvres Compared

5 min read Written by Dr. OH JUNGHO, ENT specialist Originally published in Korean: June 20, 2025 Read the original (한국어) ↗

This is a review of a 2022 study from the Department of Otorhinolaryngology at Konkuk University Medical Center, Korea. It proposes a new treatment for horizontal semicircular canal BPPV — specifically cupulolithiasis, where otoconia are attached to the cupula — and analyses how well it works.

Study: Therapeutic Maneuver for Horizontal Semicircular Canal Cupulolithiasis: A Prospective Randomized Trial. J. Clin. Med. 2022, 11, 4136. doi.org/10.3390/jcm11144136. (The figures from the paper shown in the Korean original are not reproduced here for copyright reasons.)

Background

Horizontal canal BPPV (lateral canal BPPV, HC-BPPV) is generally divided into geotropic and apogeotropic types according to the direction of the nystagmus.

Apogeotropic horizontal-canal BPPV can reflect either cupula-adherent debris or free-floating debris in the anterior (ampullary) arm. This study enrolled patients with persistent apogeotropic nystagmus consistent with presumed cupulolithiasis. Compared with BPPV elsewhere (for example the posterior canal), it responds less well to repositioning manoeuvres and often needs repeated treatment.

The study compared two methods.

Method 1: CuRM — cupulolith repositioning manoeuvre using mastoid vibration

This method, already in use, uses a vibrator to detach the otoconia and reposition them. (Originally described in Auris Nasus Larynx 2012;39(2):163–168.)

  1. Lay the patient supine. In this position there may be no nystagmus, or weak horizontal nystagmus toward the affected side.
  2. Turn the head 135° toward the affected side (position 1). The body moves from supine to lying on the affected side (lateral decubitus). A 60 Hz hand-held vibrator is then applied for 30 seconds to the area behind and above the ear canal on the affected side (suprameatal triangle) to detach otoconia attached to the cupula (especially on the canal side).
  3. Turn the head 45° toward the healthy side (position 2). The body stays lying on the affected side.
  4. Turn the head 90° toward the healthy side (position 3). The body returns to supine.
  5. Turn the head another 90° toward the healthy side (position 4). The body is now lying on the healthy side. If apogeotropic nystagmus is seen in this position, the vibrator is applied again to detach otoconia attached on the utricular side.
  6. Turn the head a further 90° in the same direction (position 5). The patient is now prone (face down).

The patient then slowly returns to sitting to finish.

The earlier CuRM report included prolonged positioning and advice to sleep on the healthy side. These historical instructions should not be confused with the 2022 trial, which gave no additional post-treatment positioning or exercise instructions. Their independent benefit was not tested here.

Method 2: the new repositioning manoeuvre

  1. Sit the patient upright in the middle of the bed.
  2. Turn the head 30° toward the affected side so the ampullary axis of the affected side lines up with the sagittal plane (position 1).
  3. Quickly lay the patient down on the healthy side and hold for 2 minutes (position 2). The initial rapid downward acceleration of the affected ampulla may detach canal-side otoconia from the cupula, and the final rapid deceleration may detach utricular-side otoconia. Waiting 2 minutes lets gravity help utricular-side otoconia fall into the utricle.
  4. Slowly return to sitting and hold for 1 minute (position 3).
  5. Turn the head 30° toward the affected side again, then quickly lie down on the affected side and hold for 2 minutes (position 4). The initial rapid downward acceleration toward the affected side may detach utricular-side otoconia from the cupula, and the final rapid deceleration detaches canal-side otoconia. Waiting 2 minutes lets canal-side otoconia fall into the canal by gravity.
  6. Quickly turn the head 45° upward and hold for 2 minutes, so otoconia in the anterior arm of the horizontal canal move into the posterior arm (position 5).
  7. Slowly return to sitting.

Results

CuRM

Of 22 patients treated with CuRM, the immediate success rate was 36.4% (8 patients). Of the 14 who were not immediately successful, 2 did not attend the first follow-up and 12 had positional nystagmus testing at the first follow-up.

In 75% of these (9 patients), no apogeotropic nystagmus was seen on the supine head-roll test: 8 had no nystagmus and 1 had geotropic nystagmus.

The 3 patients with persistent apogeotropic nystagmus were treated again with CuRM at the first follow-up, but all failed; at the second follow-up they still had apogeotropic nystagmus, and a further CuRM failed in all three.

New manoeuvre

Of 35 patients treated with the new manoeuvre, the immediate success rate was 28.6% (10 patients). Of the 25 not immediately successful, 2 did not attend the first follow-up and 23 had positional nystagmus testing.

In 82.6% of these (19 patients), no apogeotropic nystagmus was seen on the supine head-roll test: 17 had no nystagmus and 2 had geotropic nystagmus.

The 4 patients with apogeotropic nystagmus were treated again with the new manoeuvre at the first follow-up; 1 was successful and 3 were not, so a second follow-up was scheduled. At the second follow-up 1 did not attend, and the other 2 had no nystagmus on testing.

Take-home points
  • In this 57-patient trial of presumed horizontal-canal cupulolithiasis, immediate success at the initial visit was 36.4% with CuRM and 28.6% with the new manoeuvre. The visit protocol allowed a repeat attempt after initial failure, and success included conversion to transient geotropic nystagmus, not only complete disappearance of nystagmus.
  • Some cases don't resolve easily — for example because of a canalith jam (otoconia stuck within the canal) — but most studies report resolution within about 2 weeks.

For exercises that some people with BPPV do at home, see Brandt–Daroff exercises for BPPV.
